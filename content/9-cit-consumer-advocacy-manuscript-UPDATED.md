## Abstract

**Purpose:** This study aims to explore how consumers describe meaningful shopping incidents in shopping services accessed through TikTok in Indonesia, and the relationship, if any, between those incidents and consumer advocacy, non-advocacy, or negative communication.

**Design/methodology/approach:** This study employs the Critical Incident Technique (CIT) adapted for qualitative exploration. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with `[N]` adult consumers in Indonesia who had transacted via TikTok shopping services within the past 12 months. A multidimensional recording matrix was developed to capture types of response, behavioral status, incentives, specific targets, and temporal dimensions. Data were analyzed using codebook-based qualitative content analysis with inter-coder reliability assessment.

It contributes to relationship marketing in social commerce by demonstrating that brand advocacy is dynamic, non-linear, and context-dependent.

## 1. Introduction

Social commerce brings shopping into environments where consumers encounter products through social interaction, creator content, and platform features. In Indonesia, shopping accessed through TikTok involves an ecosystem in which consumers interact with services, sellers, creators, and logistics providers. `[Add current, verified sources for platform context and terminology, e.g., TikTok Shop by Tokopedia integration, Shopee Live, etc.]`

Consumer advocacy involves positive recommendations or defense of a service without financial remuneration. Yet a positive experience need not result in a recommendation, and a consumer may direct support or criticism toward an actor other than the shopping service. An incident-based approach complements survey research by examining how users narrate specific experiences, actions, and their interpretations (Flanagan, 1954; Gremler, 2004).

Existing literature faces two limitations: (1) studies often treat platform, merchants, and creators as a monolith, creating ambiguity regarding the actual object of brand advocacy; (2) there is an oversimplified assumption that positive experiences linearly result in advocacy. `[Replace this provisional gap with a precise, cited comparison of relevant studies.]`

This study addresses the following research questions:

**RQ1:** What meaningful critical incidents do users describe in shopping through TikTok in Indonesia, and to whom do they attribute responsibility?

**RQ2:** How do users explain the relationship, if any, between these incidents and advocacy, non-advocacy, or negative communication toward the shopping service?

## 2. Literature Review and Theoretical Orientation

### 2.1 Social Commerce and the Shopping-Service Context

Social commerce is a distinct evolution from traditional electronic commerce by embedding transactional capabilities within social media environments. Rather than deliberate, intent-based search, social commerce encourages discovery-based consumption, frequently conceptualized as "shoppertainment." `[Insert verified sources and publication details.]`

The shopping service integrated into TikTok merges immersive algorithmic video loops, real-time live broadcasting, and instantaneous checkout functionalities. This environment fosters high emotional resonance and dynamic interpersonal communication, altering how consumer satisfaction and platform evaluations are generated.

### 2.2 Consumer Advocacy, Non-Advocacy, and Incentives

**Actual advocacy** refers to a participant-reported positive recommendation or defense directed toward the shopping service without an identified incentive. Advocacy intention is recorded separately from actual conduct.

**Non-advocacy** refers to the absence of recommendation or defense in the specified context or period, whether the experience is positive, neutral, or negative.

**Negative communication** is a comparator, not positive advocacy. Incentivized communication and unknown incentive status are recorded separately; motivation cannot be inferred solely from an incentive. `[Support the working definitions with relevant literature.]`

### 2.3 Attribution of Responsibility as an Interpretive Lens

Attribution of responsibility serves as the primary interpretive lens to explore which actors participants regard as responsible and why. In a social commerce transaction, multiple independent actors are involved: the core platform (TikTok/Tokopedia), independent merchants, content creators/affiliates, and third-party logistics providers. `[Identify and cite the precise attribution theory source and specify dimensions used, e.g., locus of causality, stability, controllability.]`

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Research Design and Unit of Analysis

The study uses an exploratory qualitative design adapting the Critical Incident Technique (Flanagan, 1954; Gremler, 2004). The principal unit of analysis is a specific incident, not the participant. One participant may describe multiple incidents.

A critical incident is a specific experience for which context, actors, sequence, and outcome can be identified, and which the participant considers meaningful to their views, decisions, or responses concerning the service.

The focal object is the shopping service accessed through TikTok in Indonesia as understood by the participant. Source of experience, actor regarded as responsible, and target of communication are recorded separately.

### 3.2 Sampling and Eligibility

Participants were purposively recruited to seek variation in positive, negative, and mixed experiences, as well as advocacy, non-advocacy, and negative communication.

**Eligibility criteria:** age at least 18; residence in Indonesia; at least one transaction through the service in the preceding 12 months; willingness to describe a meaningful specific experience.

**Data collection period:** `[Insert actual dates, e.g., December 2024 – February 2025]`

**Mode:** `[Online / In-person]`, language: `[Indonesian]`

Recruitment used `[approved channels]`, subject to ethics review. A provisional range of 20–30 interviews was planned; actual number depended on data adequacy (depth, incident diversity, comparison cases). `[Record recruitment steps, changes, and stopping rationale.]`

### 3.3 Data Collection Procedure

Semi-structured interviews were conducted (average 45–60 minutes) after informed consent and recording permission. A pilot examined clarity, neutrality, and ability to elicit concrete incidents.

**Interview protocol covered:** what happened, when, which actors were involved, what each did, current outcome, why the incident mattered, perceived responsibility, and whether any recommendation, criticism, silence, or other response followed. Interviewers asked whether participants perceived any connection, without presuming causality.

Candidate incidents were logged and assessed for relevance, specificity, sufficient context/action/outcome, and participant-perceived significance. General opinions without an identifiable event, insufficiently detailed accounts, out-of-scope accounts, and confirmed duplicates were excluded with reasons. Lack of an actual communication response was not grounds for exclusion.

### 3.4 Data Analysis and Reliability

Codebook-based qualitative content analysis was used. The initial codebook was informed by research questions, relevant concepts, and exploratory reading of incidents. Two coders independently classified a prespecified subset using a documented codebook version. A single incident could receive multiple codes for response, target, timing, or incentive status.

**Cohen's Kappa** was calculated for clearly defined mutually exclusive categories or as present/absent for suitable individual codes. `[Report: subset selection, number of units, category distribution, observed agreement, kappa where computable.]`

Deviant cases and incidents without advocacy were examined, with decisions documented in an audit trail.

### 3.5 Ethics and Data Management

Data collection began only after required institutional ethics approval and informed consent. `[Specify approval number and date.]`

Participants could stop without penalty. Consent materials stated a 7-day deadline for requesting removal of identifiable data. Recordings, transcripts, and identifying keys were stored with restricted access for the period required by institutional policy.

Member-checking was conducted by providing full transcripts to participants for verification within 7 days.
